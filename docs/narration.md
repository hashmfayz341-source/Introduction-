## 1. A cell in balance

A living cell constantly balances energy, ions, proteins, and its surroundings. Homeostasis is this active maintenance of a stable internal environment.

Its membrane controls exchange, mitochondria supply energy, and the nucleus stores genetic instructions. Other organelles build, transport, or break down cellular materials.

Labile cells divide regularly. Stable cells usually rest but can divide after stimulation. Adult permanent cells, such as most neurons, have very limited proliferative capacity.

Each cell has different limits. Its type, condition, and function help determine how much stress it can tolerate, and how it responds.

## 2. Adapt before injury

An increased demand does not automatically injure a cell. Within its limits, adaptation creates a new steady state that preserves viability and function.

Hypertrophy increases cell size. A heart muscle cell can enlarge when workload rises. Atrophy reduces cell size, and sometimes cell number, when demand or support falls.

Hyperplasia increases the number of cells in tissues able to proliferate. Metaplasia replaces one differentiated cell type with another better suited to the stress.

But adaptation has limits. When an insult exceeds the cell's capacity, or directly threatens its structure and function, the story changes from adaptation to injury.

## 3. Recover or cross the threshold

Stress is the challenge; injury is the harmful effect on the cell. The outcome depends on the insult's type, intensity, and duration, together with cellular susceptibility.

Reversible injury means function and morphology can recover if the damaging stimulus is removed soon enough. Swelling and membrane blebs may already be present.

Crucially, functional membrane integrity and nuclear viability are preserved. Reversible injury does not mean that every membrane structure remains perfectly unchanged.

Irreversible injury develops when recovery becomes impossible. A very severe insult can reach that point rapidly; removing the cause after this threshold cannot rescue the cell.

## 4. Different insults, shared targets

The lecture emphasizes oxygen deprivation. Hypoxia is insufficient oxygen; ischemia is reduced blood flow, which also limits nutrients and removal of metabolic waste.

Physical injury includes sharp and blunt trauma. Chemicals include acids, alkalis, toxins, and pollutants. Biological causes include infections and parasitic infestations.

Environmental extremes include high or low temperature and radiation. Both excesses and deficiencies of major nutrients or micronutrients can also injure cells.

Genetic abnormalities alter function or susceptibility. The source lists Down syndrome, sickle cell anemia, and Mediterranean disease. Aging adds declining cellular reserve and repair capacity.

## 5. Inside the vulnerable cell

Many different insults converge on a small set of targets. Mitochondria are central because cells need ATP to maintain their essential functions.

Cell membranes maintain the boundary between compartments. Losing this control disturbs ion balance and allows substances to enter or leave inappropriately.

The endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes support protein synthesis. The cytoskeleton supplies internal support and helps preserve the cell's shape and membrane organization.

The genetic apparatus, especially DNA, preserves the instructions needed for survival. These targets interact: injury to one can amplify damage to the others.

## 6. When the energy supply falls

Follow a clot blocking a vessel. Downstream blood flow falls, so the cell receives less oxygen and fewer nutrients. This is the setting for ischemic injury.

Without adequate oxygen, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation slows. ATP production falls, leaving less energy available for membrane pumps and other essential work.

Reduced nutrients, direct mitochondrial damage, and toxins such as cyanide can also deplete ATP. Oxygen delivery alone is not the only point where energy production can fail.

Severe, sustained ATP depletion is a major mechanism of necrosis. Early energy failure, however, can still produce reversible changes. Watch what happens to ion transport first.

## 7. ATP falls. Water follows sodium.

The sodium potassium pump needs ATP. As ATP falls, the pump slows, and the cell can no longer maintain its usual sodium and potassium gradients.

Sodium accumulates inside, while potassium is lost. Water follows the increased intracellular solute load, producing cellular swelling.

The endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria can swell too. The cell surface may form blebs and lose microvilli. These changes are early signs of disturbed homeostasis.

Keep calcium separate in your mental model. Sodium potassium pump failure does not directly transport calcium inward; failing calcium regulation and membrane injury raise cytosolic calcium.

## 8. A temporary energy detour

With oxidative phosphorylation impaired, the cell increases anaerobic glycolysis. This provides some ATP, but far less energy than normal aerobic metabolism.

Glycogen stores are consumed as the cell draws on its fuel reserves. Lactate accumulates, and intracellular pH falls.

Acidosis alters enzyme activity and contributes to clumping of nuclear chromatin. The nucleus can still be viable at this reversible stage.

Do not treat low pH as an automatic DNA break. DNA damage is a separate injury mechanism; acidosis mainly signals disturbed metabolism and impaired cellular function here.

## 9. The protein workshop slows

Now zoom into rough endoplasmic reticulum. Its attached ribosomes normally assemble proteins that the cell needs for secretion, membranes, and other functions.

During hypoxic injury, the endoplasmic reticulum swells and ribosomes detach. Protein synthesis decreases, weakening the cell's ability to maintain and replace essential components.

Think of ATP depletion as several parallel branches: pump failure causes swelling, anaerobic glycolysis consumes reserves and lowers pH, and ribosome detachment reduces protein production.

These changes can improve if the insult ends early and energy production returns. Persistent injury gives these interacting disturbances more time to undermine recovery.

## 10. Calcium activates damage

Calcium is normally kept low in the cytosol. Ischemia and toxins can increase entry from outside and release calcium from intracellular stores.

Phospholipases break down membrane phospholipids. Proteases damage cellular proteins, including the cytoskeleton. Both weaken the cell's structure and membranes.

Endonucleases damage DNA, while ATPases consume ATP and worsen energy depletion. One calcium disturbance can therefore attack several survival systems at once.

Calcium overload also promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and can help activate apoptotic pathways involving caspases. The consequences depend on the extent and context of injury.

## 11. Two mitochondrial consequences

Ischemia, excess cytosolic calcium, and reactive oxygen species can damage mitochondria. Two consequences must stay distinct.

Increased permeability of the inner mitochondrial membrane collapses the electrochemical gradient needed for oxidative phosphorylation. ATP generation fails, favoring necrotic death when the injury is severe.

Permeabilization of the outer mitochondrial membrane can release cytochrome c into the cytosol. This helps activate caspases and the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.

These are distinct membrane events, although they can coexist during injury. Mitochondrial damage is therefore a crossroads between energy failure and programmed cell death.

## 12. Oxidative stress attacks three targets

Reactive oxygen species include superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, but also hydrogen peroxide, which is not a free radical. Excess production overwhelms cellular defenses.

Radiation, toxins, and damaged mitochondria can increase ROS. The source also highlights reperfusion: restoring blood flow can add oxidative injury to previously ischemic tissue.

Lipid peroxidation damages membranes. Protein oxidation can cause breakdown or misfolding. DNA injury can produce mutations or trigger death pathways if the damage cannot be repaired.

Oxidative stress is a balance problem: damage rises when reactive species outpace their removal. Defenses matter as much as production.

## 13. Keep oxidants under control

Some reactive species decay spontaneously. Superoxide dismutase converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide; this is an intermediate step, not complete detoxification.

Catalase and glutathione peroxidase then remove hydrogen peroxide. Glutathione participates in cellular antioxidant defense, including the liver, rather than acting only there.

The lecture also lists carotenoids, vitamins E and C, and flavonoids. Selenium and zinc support antioxidant systems; they should not be pictured as directly swallowing every radical.

These defenses can be overwhelmed. Their normal biological roles do not mean that taking supplements will reverse established ischemic injury or prevent every kind of cell damage.

## 14. Boundaries begin to fail

Energy failure, oxidative damage, and calcium activated enzymes converge on membranes. Reduced phospholipid synthesis, lipid breakdown, and cytoskeletal damage weaken their structure.

Loss of plasma membrane integrity causes uncontrolled exchange and leakage of cell contents. Unlike a reversible surface bleb, major membrane disruption threatens cell survival.

Mitochondrial membrane damage further impairs energy production. Lysosomal membrane injury releases digestive enzymes into the cytoplasm, where they can degrade cellular contents.

This creates a damaging cycle: energy failure and enzyme activation injure membranes; failed membranes deepen ion disturbances and organelle injury. Necrosis becomes increasingly likely.

## 15. Repair, or activate apoptosis

Radiation, chemicals, and ROS can damage DNA. Cells can repair limited damage, so a damaged molecule does not automatically mean a doomed cell.

If repair succeeds, survival remains possible. If severe DNA damage cannot be corrected, the cell can activate apoptosis rather than continue with an unsafe genome.

Accumulated damaged or misfolded proteins can also stress the cell and activate apoptotic pathways. This connects the lecture's protein and genetic injury targets.

Caspases carry out the organized dismantling of apoptosis. This is different from the uncontrolled leakage and digestion that dominate the necrotic pattern.

## 16. Two patterns of cell death

Necrosis commonly features swelling, severe organelle damage, and loss of membrane integrity. Cellular contents leak and can provoke inflammation in surrounding tissue.

The lecture's morphology diagram shows a condensed nucleus followed by fragmentation, disrupted membranes, lysosomal rupture, and amorphous densities in swollen mitochondria.

Apoptosis is organized: the cell shrinks, chromatin condenses, and the cell breaks into membrane bounded fragments. These can be removed with relatively little inflammation.

These are useful patterns, not rigid labels for every injured cell. The lecture's focus is how energy, membranes, calcium, ROS, DNA, and proteins lead toward them.

## 17. Return to the same cell

Return to our swollen cell. Restore oxygen and support early enough, and ATP production, ion gradients, cell volume, and protein synthesis can recover.

Swelling, blebs, and chromatin clumping can resolve. Severe irreversible damage brings membrane fragmentation, nuclear destruction, and membrane derived myelin figures instead.

Two major hallmarks of irreversible injury are an inability to reverse mitochondrial dysfunction and profound membrane damage. Removal of the original stimulus is then too late.

The lesson is timing plus severity plus cell type. Adaptation, recovery, and death are outcomes of the cell's response to its environment, not a fixed clock.

## 18. One connected injury network

Remember the energy chain: ATP falls, ion pumps fail, sodium accumulates, water enters, and the cell swells. Glycolysis and reduced protein synthesis add parallel consequences.

Calcium activates damaging enzymes. ROS attack lipids, proteins, and DNA. Mitochondrial injury undermines ATP or releases apoptotic signals; membrane injury removes essential boundaries.

Unrepaired DNA and protein damage can activate apoptosis. These mechanisms reinforce one another, so understand their connections rather than memorizing six isolated boxes.

Recoverable dysfunction is reversible injury. Irreparable mitochondrial and membrane failure marks irreversible injury. Always ask: what failed, why did it fail, and can this cell still recover?
